Before you call a hotline

Information, not diagnosis or treatment. This guide was drafted from the sources listed at the end and has not yet been reviewed by a qualified mental health adviser. Review status: pending. Last updated 2026-10-25. If you are in danger right now, go to Immediate Help.

People put off calling because they don't know what will happen. Here's what will happen.

Who answers

On the NCMH Crisis Hotline, a responder from the National Center for Mental Health picks up. The line is open 24 hours a day, every day. Speak in whatever language is easier for you, and give only what you're comfortable giving.

What they'll ask

  • What's going on right now, and whether you're safe at this moment.
  • Whether you've thought about hurting yourself, and whether you have a plan or means nearby. Honest answers get you the most useful help.
  • Who's around you, and what has helped before.

What you can say

You don't need a reason that sounds big enough. "Hindi ko alam kung bakit ako tumawag, pero hindi ako okay" is a complete opening line. Crying, silence, and starting over are all fine.

Have nearby, if you can

  • A charged phone and a quiet corner.
  • Water. A blanket. Something to hold.
  • The name of one person you could message afterward.

If you can't get through

  • Try the next number on the Immediate Help page. Busy lines mean others are calling too.
  • If you're in danger, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room.
  • Tell one person near you that you need them to stay with you for the next hour.

After the call

Write down anything they suggested while it's fresh. If they pointed you to a service, book it the same day if you can. Calling once makes calling again easier.

Sources: Republic Act No. 11036, Mental Health Act of 2018 (Section 21 requires 24/7 crisis hotlines); Daily Tribune, "DOH mental health hotline gets nearly 1K calls during holiday season" (3 January 2026).
