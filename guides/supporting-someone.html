How to support someone who's struggling

Information, not diagnosis or treatment. This guide was drafted from the sources listed at the end and has not yet been reviewed by a qualified mental health adviser. Review status: pending. Last updated 2026-10-25. If you are in danger right now, go to Immediate Help.

You don't need training to be a good first person. You need to show up, listen, ask directly, and know the numbers.

Listen first

  • Open with what you noticed, not a verdict: "Napansin ko lang, parang mabigat ka these days. Okay ka lang ba?"
  • Let silence sit. Don't rush to fix, cheer up, or compare ("ako nga dati…").
  • Reflect back: "So what I'm hearing is…" People calm down when they feel understood.
  • Don't diagnose. You can say "this sounds really hard" without naming a condition.

Ask the hard question

If you're worried, ask plainly: "Naiisip mo bang saktan ang sarili mo?" Asking directly does not plant the idea. It opens the door. If the answer is yes, stay calm, stay with them, and move to the next section.

When to call, and how

  • If they're in danger right now: call 911 or the NCMH Crisis Hotline together, or go with them to the nearest emergency room. Don't leave them alone.
  • If it's serious but not immediate: help them pick a professional from Find Support and sit with them while they book.
  • Put means out of reach for now: medicines, sharp objects, anything they mentioned.

Keep showing up

Check in a few days later without an agenda. Invite them to small things. Keep your own limits: you're a friend, not their therapist, and you're allowed to ask someone for help carrying this too.

Sources: World Health Organization. (2020). Doing what matters in times of stress: An illustrated guide; Republic Act No. 11036, Mental Health Act of 2018; NCMH Crisis Hotline numbers as confirmed on the Immediate Help page.
